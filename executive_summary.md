# Do Families Face Higher Inflation?
### An Executive Summary for Leaders and Managers

**Project overview:** A data-driven investigation into whether the spending mix of American households with children produced higher inflation than that of single-person and other households during the 2020 to 2026 inflation surge. Revised September 25, 2026 after an uncertainty-method correction; earlier confidence intervals are superseded.

---

## 1. Key findings

* **The core question:** Did families' mix of purchases produce a higher inflation rate because they spend more on groceries, childcare, vehicles and education?
* **The main finding: No meaningful excess in this index.** From December 2019 to July 2026, the common-CPI-priced basket rose **29.4%** for families with children and **30.1%** for single-person and other households. The family gap was **-0.7 percentage points over six and a half years**, or roughly 0.1 point a year.
* **The bigger point: these group baskets have similar inflation.** Compared with all households, the family basket rose 0.2 points less. Compared with married couples without children, it rose 0.2 points more. Neither difference is distinguishable from zero. Whether families look "lower" depends mostly on who they are compared with.
* **Why:** About half the measured gap comes from housing. The single-person-and-other group spends more of its basket on shelter (36% against 30% for families), and shelter prices rose faster than most other prices. This comparison does not isolate the effect of children from age, income or tenure.
* **How confident we are:** Across nine baskets, the family-minus-single/other gap ranges from -1.0 to +0.1 points; none puts families meaningfully above. It is about -0.5 rather than -0.7 with more current spending weights. The corrected 95% sampling margin from the disjoint-cell check is ±0.41 points if errors are independent across survey years and ±0.95 points under the cross-year bound; the latter includes zero.
* **Rate versus dollar cost:** Holding each group's 2019 non-housing basket fixed, buying it at July 2026 prices costs a married couple with children about **$16,300 more per year**, against about **$8,100** for a single-person-and-other household. This is a basket-cost comparison, not a measure of hardship.

---

## 2. What we did

Headline inflation (the Consumer Price Index, or CPI) measures price change for urban consumers' spending basket. We built separate inflation measures for different household types:

1. **What people buy.** Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Expenditure Survey data give the share of each group's budget spent across 41 categories (groceries, childcare, vehicles, rent, electricity and so on).
2. **What those things cost.** Each category is matched to its official monthly CPI price index.
3. **Housing done the way CPI does it.** CPI measures homeowners' housing costs by what their home would rent for. We used the survey's own question on the rental value of owned homes, the same question BLS uses, so homeowners and renters are treated consistently. Our all-households version of this index tracks the official CPI closely.
4. **How precise the answer is.** We propagated BLS's published expenditure standard errors into approximate sampling intervals for the comparisons.
5. **Checks against BLS's own work.** BLS publishes a research inflation index by income group. Our method finds a similar cumulative gap for 2019 to 2025, though its annual results and earlier years differ.

## 3. Detailed findings

### Finding 1: The family basket rose slightly less than the single/other basket

| December 2019 to July 2026 | Families with children | Single-person and other households | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **All items, CPI-like method** | **29.4%** | **30.1%** | **-0.7 points** (95% sampling margin 0.41 to 0.95) |
| Excluding housing | 27.5% | 27.9% | -0.4 points (within the margin of error) |

Across nine different ways of defining the basket, the difference ranged from -1.0 to +0.1 points. No version shows families meaningfully worse off on the rate. The margin of error depends on how much the survey's errors carry over from one year to the next; under the most cautious assumption the -0.7 is not statistically distinguishable from zero.

### Finding 2: The answer depends on the comparison

| Families with children compared with | Difference in inflation, 2019 to 2026 |
|---|---|
| Single-person and other households | -0.7 points |
| All other households | -0.3 points |
| All households | -0.2 points |
| Married couples without children | +0.2 points |

Only the first clears the usual margin of error when survey-year errors are assumed independent; none does under the cross-year bound. Families look lower against single-person-and-other households because that group spends more of its budget on housing, which rose fastest. Against childless married couples, who are older and spend more on medical care, which rose slowly, families come out slightly higher. The observed group-basket differences are a few tenths of a point either way.

### Finding 3: The 2021-22 surge was not clearly worse for families

* **2021 to 2022:** Families' 12-month inflation ran slightly above single households' for a while, up to about 0.6 points at the peak. Over the two years as a whole, the difference was +0.2 points, which is not statistically distinguishable from zero.
* **2023 to 2024:** Singles ran clearly hotter, by about 0.65 points over the two years, as rents caught up, hitting renters, who are disproportionately single households.
* **By July 2026:** The gap was back at zero.

### Finding 4: Why childcare did not push the family rate up

Families spend about eight times as much of their budget on childcare as single households (1.6% against 0.2%); for families with a child under 18 it is about twelve times. But childcare prices rose 29.8% since 2019, the same as overall inflation (29.9%), so the extra spending did not change the family rate at all. Childcare prices rise at about the same pace as other services. The problem with childcare is how big the bill is: married couples whose oldest child is under 6 spend about $5,600 a year on it on average, and much more if they pay for full-time care.

### Finding 5: Income matters more than family type

The same method shows a much larger gap by income:

| Comparison, December 2019 to July 2026 | Difference in inflation |
|---|---|
| **Lowest-income 10% vs highest-income 10%** | **+2.1 points** |
| **Lowest-income 20% vs highest-income 20%** | **+1.8 points** |
| Families with children vs single-person households | -0.7 points |

Lower-income households faced higher inflation mainly because more of their budget goes to rent, which rose faster than most other prices. BLS's own research index by income group shows a similar gap over the same period (+2.1 points for the lowest versus highest 20%, December 2019 to December 2025).

### Finding 6: Similar rates imply different dollar increases for different basket sizes

| Household group | Inflation (CPI method) | Extra dollars per year to buy the 2019 non-housing basket | Share of spending on food at home, utilities and housing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Married couple with children** | 29.3% | **$16,300** | 36.6% |
| **Single person and other** | 30.1% | $8,100 | 43.3% |
| **Single parent** | 30.2% | $9,500 | 43.0% |
| **Lowest-income 20%** | 30.5% | $5,800 | 46.3% |

1. **Bigger baskets mean bigger dollar increases.** The cost increase for married families' fixed 2019 non-housing basket is about twice that for the single/other group, at a slightly lower rate. The figure does not account for changes in quantities or household size.
2. **Single parents have a higher necessity share.** Their index rose about 0.9 points more than married parents' (statistically clear only under the independent-year assumption), and they devote a larger share of spending to basic needs. The index alone does not measure hardship.

---

## 4. Takeaways

1. **Be precise about "family inflation."** Differences in the measured spending mix do not show a meaningful family excess over this period, including during the 2021-22 surge. This index does not capture differences in prices paid within items.
2. **Examine income and housing exposure.** Their observed index gaps are larger than the family-versus-single/other gap; the published tables do not isolate a causal effect of children.
3. **Talk about dollars and rates.** Families' larger baskets imply larger fixed-basket dollar increases even at similar rates. Childcare is a problem of the size of the bill, not of its inflation rate in this period.
4. **"Families" is not one group.** Single-parent households look more like lower-income households than like married couples with children.
